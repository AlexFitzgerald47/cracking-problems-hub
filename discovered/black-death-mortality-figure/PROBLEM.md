# Where Does the Black Death's Mortality Figure Actually Come From?

## Statement
That the Black Death killed something between a third and a half of Europe's population is
one of the most-repeated quantitative claims in history teaching. There was no census. The
figure rests on manorial records, wills, ecclesiastical vacancy rates and a small set of
much-quoted chronicler impressions, generalised to a continent.

In 2022 a palynological study argued that mortality was in fact **wildly heterogeneous by
region** — catastrophic in some areas, barely detectable in others — explicitly against what
its authors described as growing acceptance of a roughly 50 per cent figure. It drew a
published critique and a published rebuttal in the same journal. The open problem has two
halves: **where the received number came from**, and **whether the aggregate figure means
anything at all** if the regional variance is as large as the pollen data suggests.

## Corpus status — read this first
**Partial.** The comparative estimates from documentary and economic history are available
and can be collated and compared today with no new data collection. The 2022 palynological
dataset (1,634 radiocarbon-dated pollen samples from 261 sites across 19 countries) is
subject to *Nature*'s data-availability requirements and is very likely deposited in a public
repository — **but the repository and DOI were not confirmed during this run and must be
located before any re-analysis is scoped.** Do not assume it is trivially downloadable.

## Why it belongs on the board
The Black Death is a household name; the evidentiary basis of its headline number is not,
and the methodological fight between palynology and documentary demography is squarely
specialist. This clears the obscurity bar on the angle rather than on the subject, and the
brief permits exactly that where the angle is genuinely unworked — the citation history of
the figure itself has not, as far as this run could establish, been systematically traced.

The 2022 exchange also makes this unusually well-conditioned: two named methods, in open
disagreement, in print, with the disagreement unresolved.

- **Critique: "Reduction in grain pollen indicates population decline, but not necessarily
  Black Death mortality," *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 6 (2022), pp. 1626–1627,** DOI
  10.1038/s41559-022-01862-4. *Verified.*
- **Reply: Izdebski et al., "Reply to 'Reduction in grain pollen…'," *Nature Ecology &
  Evolution* 6:11 (2022), pp. 1628–1629,** DOI 10.1038/s41559-022-01863-3. *Verified.*
  The critique-and-reply pair is the evidence that this is live and adversarial, not settled.
- **Remi Jedwab, Noel D. Johnson and Mark Koyama** — a synthesis of Western European
  demographic data giving a **mean estimate of 38.75 per cent** mortality. *Figure verified;
  venue only partly so.* These authors have several overlapping Black Death papers, and the
  one carrying this figure was not pinned down: candidates include "Pandemics, Places, and
  Populations: Evidence from the Black Death" (SSRN 3331972; a related version appears in
  the *Journal of Urban Economics* 139, 2024) and "Negative shocks and mass persecutions:
  evidence from the Black Death", *Journal of Economic Growth* 24:4 (Dec 2019), pp. 345–395.
  **Establish which paper carries the 38.75 per cent estimate before citing it** — an
  instructive small instance of the very failure mode this problem studies.
- **John Aberth**'s documentary estimate of 51–58 per cent. *Unverified — neither figure nor
  venue confirmed this run.*
- **Ole Benedictow**'s high estimates are the other pole of the documentary literature.
  *Named from general knowledge; not verified this run.*

## Success criteria
1. A traced citation lineage of the received "one-third" or "one-half" figure: the earliest
   chronicle sources behind it, the point at which impressionistic statements were converted
   into a demographic percentage, and the intermediaries through which it reached modern
   textbooks. This is the core deliverable and is fully tractable from digitised material.
2. A reconciliation table of every major modern estimate, each tagged with its method,
   geographic coverage, and what it actually measures — because several of these numbers are
   not estimating the same quantity and are routinely compared as if they were.
3. A defensible statement on whether a single European aggregate figure is meaningful given
   the claimed regional variance, or whether the honest output of the field is a distribution.

## Key sources & starting points
- Izdebski et al. (2022), the critique, and the reply — all three verified as above.
- Internet Archive, HathiTrust and Google Books for the nineteenth- and twentieth-century
  intermediaries through which the figure propagated. This is where the citation-chain work
  actually happens.

## Notes
Difficulty: high on the substance, moderate on the citation history. Tractability with
text/compute alone: **very good for the citation-chain half, poor for the palynology.**

**Time-waster warning.** Do not attempt to adjudicate the true mortality rate. That requires
palynological expertise this Hub cannot manufacture, and an agent who tries will produce an
opinion dressed as an analysis. The tractable, valuable and unclaimed work is the citation
archaeology and the reconciliation table — establishing what each number is, where it came
from, and what it measures. Stay there.

Second trap: treating the 2022 paper as having overturned the consensus. It was contested in
print within months, and the reply did not end the argument. Report the exchange as an open
disagreement, which is what it is.
