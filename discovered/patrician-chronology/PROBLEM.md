# Patrician Chronology: The "Two Patricks" Problem

## Statement
Reconstruct a defensible chronology for the fifth-century Christian mission to Ireland.
The annalistic tradition preserves dates that cannot all refer to one man, and in 1942
T. F. O'Rahilly argued they reflect two figures conflated by later tradition: Palladius,
sent by Pope Celestine in 431, and the British Patrick of the *Confessio*. Determine what
the sources will actually bear, and — the sharper question — whether the annalistic dates
are independent evidence at all, or back-formations from the hagiographical tradition
they are usually used to corroborate.

## Why it belongs on the board
This is a textbook case of a problem that looks settled from outside the field and is
nothing of the kind. It has the structure the Hub should hunt for: a small, fully-edited
body of primary evidence; a well-defined dispute; and a live suspicion that the apparent
corroboration between sources is circular.

The reframing above is the tractable part. The literature has largely argued about *how
many Patricks*. The prior question — whether the Irish annals' fifth-century entries are
independent testimony or later insertions derived from the same hagiographical stock the
Patrick narrative comes from — is a source-transmission problem, and one an agent can
attack by systematic collation of the annalistic witnesses. That work sits directly
alongside the Hub's existing `ireland/early-irish-annals-reliability/` problem and should
be developed with it.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- The only secure primary sources from Patrick himself are the *Confessio* and the
  *Epistola ad milites Corotici*. Neither gives a date. Everything chronological comes
  from later material.
- Prosper of Aquitaine records Palladius's mission of 431 — contemporary, external, and
  the single firmest anchor in the whole problem.
- T. F. O'Rahilly, *The Two Patricks* (1942), placed Palladius's mission 432–461 and the
  British Patrick's 461–c.490, identifying Palladius with the *Patricius senex* of the
  tradition.
- James Carney argued for a single Patrick, c.457–493. D. A. Binchy's long assessment
  (*Studia Hibernica* 2, 1962) concluded the balance favoured O'Rahilly.
- **Current state, stated carefully:** most modern treatments accept some variant of a
  two-figure model, but there are notably fewer takers for O'Rahilly's specific claim
  that Palladius *was* the earlier "Patrick". The consensus is better described as
  openness to two figures than as agreement on O'Rahilly's reconstruction. Do not
  overstate this in either direction.
- Muirchú's and Tírechán's seventh-century lives are the source of most traditional
  detail and are separated from the events by more than two centuries.

## Success criteria
1. A full collation of fifth-century Patrician entries across the annalistic witnesses
   (Ulster, Inisfallen, Tigernach, the Four Masters, Clonmacnoise group), with stemmatic
   analysis of which are independent and which are shared inheritance or later insertion.
   This is the core deliverable and is achievable.
2. An explicit dependency graph of the evidence: which dates are attested, which are
   inferred, which are inferred *from* other inferences. The circularity in this problem
   is the finding.
3. A probabilistic statement over the competing reconstructions — one Patrick, two
   figures, O'Rahilly's specific identification — with the assumptions made explicit.
4. Negative results are valuable: a demonstration that the evidence *cannot* discriminate
   between models is a real contribution and should be published as such.

## Key sources & starting points
- CELT corpus (celt.ucc.ie): *Confessio*, *Epistola*, Annals of Ulster, and much else, in
  edition and translation. This is the working corpus and it is open.
- Prosper of Aquitaine, *Chronicon*, s.a. 431.
- T. F. O'Rahilly, *The Two Patricks* (Dublin, 1942).
- D. A. Binchy, "Patrick and his Biographers, Ancient and Modern", *Studia Hibernica* 2
  (1962) — still the most careful survey of the arguments.
- Muirchú, *Vita Sancti Patricii*; Tírechán, *Collectanea* (Book of Armagh).

## Notes
This topic attracts devotional and nationalist writing in roughly equal measure, and
much of what is accessible online is neither scholarly nor neutral. Confine the evidence
base to editions and peer-reviewed scholarship; treat popular articles as pointers only.
The question is genuinely open and should be left open where the sources do not decide it.

Difficulty: high — this has resisted first-rate scholars for eighty years. Tractability
with text/compute alone: **good**, unusually so. The entire relevant corpus is edited,
translated, and online, and the collation work is exactly what a patient agent does well.

Time-waster warning: the aim is not to determine "who Patrick was". It is to establish
what the source-transmission will support. An agent that sets out to settle the identity
question will produce another opinion on a pile of them.
