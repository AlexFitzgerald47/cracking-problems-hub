# Zapotec Hieroglyphic Writing: Mesoamerica's Other Script

## Statement
The Zapotec script of Monte Albán and the Valley of Oaxaca (c. 500 BC – AD 900) is one of
the earliest scribal traditions in Mesoamerica and has one of the longest developmental
trajectories. It is also, unlike Maya, genuinely undeciphered as connected text. Scholars
do not agree on the system's basic type — whether it is predominantly syllabic or
logophonic — and no reading of continuous Zapotec text comparable to the Maya decipherment
exists.

The open problem is to establish what the corpus itself can support: sign inventory, sign
frequency, positional distribution, and readings testable across independent occurrences.

## Corpus status — read this first
**Exists, systematised, and freely downloadable.** *Verified:* Javier Urcid Serrano,
*Zapotec Hieroglyphic Writing*, Studies in Pre-Columbian Art and Archaeology No. 34,
Dumbarton Oaks Research Library and Collection, Washington DC, 2001; xviii + 487 pp.,
**338 figures, 54 tables**, ISBN 0-88402-267-6. Confirmed across Cambridge Core review
listings in both *Latin American Antiquity* and *Antiquity*, and Dumbarton Oaks' own
publications page. **A free full copy is on the Internet Archive** at
`archive.org/details/serrano-zapotec-hieroglyphic-writing`.

*Correction on record:* the researching agent described the corpus as roughly 1,200 line
drawings. The figure count confirmed by the reviews is **338 figures and 54 tables**. The
1,200 number may count individual monuments or drawings within composite figures, but it is
**unverified** and should not be repeated until checked against the volume.

The corpus is images and drawings, not structured data. Turning it into a machine-readable
sign catalogue is the first task and does not currently appear to have been done openly.

## Why it belongs on the board
The board holds Epi-Olmec/Isthmian already; Zapotec is a separate and considerably larger
Oaxacan tradition, routinely conflated with Maya by general audiences and carefully
distinguished by specialists. Maya is excluded from this run as substantially deciphered;
Zapotec is the neighbouring tradition where the decipherment did not happen.

It is also a good structural match for the Hub's existing holdings: like `proto-elamite/`,
the realistic near-term goal is not translation but recovering structure from distribution.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- **Urcid's 2001 volume explicitly does not claim a decipherment.** It is a corpus and a
  methodological foundation, and it deliberately prioritises internal comparison within the
  Zapotec tradition over importing readings from Maya epigraphy. Respect that choice: the
  temptation to read Zapotec through Maya is exactly what Urcid built the corpus to avoid.
- The no-bilingual problem applies here as it does to Meroitic.
- Periodic optimism that Zapotec decipherment is "on the horizon" has been expressed since
  at least 2009. *Whether any of it was borne out was not established this run* — check the
  recent *Ancient Mesoamerica* literature before assuming the field is where 2001 left it.

## Success criteria
1. A machine-readable sign catalogue derived from Urcid's corpus: canonical signs, variants,
   attestations, and provenance for each. This is the enabling artefact and nothing else is
   possible without it.
2. Sign-frequency and positional-distribution analysis across the corpus — the standard
   first move on an undeciphered script, and one that, as far as this run could establish,
   has not been published as an open dataset for Zapotec.
3. Any proposed reading of a glyph or compound tested for consistency across **multiple
   independent occurrences**, by Urcid's own internal-comparison method. Consistency across
   attestations is the falsifiability criterion.
4. A defensible, evidenced position on the syllabic-versus-logophonic question, or a
   demonstration that the corpus is too small to decide it. Either is a result.

## Key sources & starting points
- Urcid (2001) — verified, free on the Internet Archive. This is the whole starting point.
- Cambridge Core reviews in *Latin American Antiquity* and *Antiquity* — for an independent
  read on what the volume does and does not establish.
- Recent *Ancient Mesoamerica* issues — the likeliest home of anything post-2001. Expect
  paywalls.

## Notes
Difficulty: high. Tractability with text/compute alone: **good for corpus and distributional
analysis, poor for decipherment.** Scope accordingly and the problem is productive; scope it
as decipherment and it is not.

**Time-waster warning.** The field runs on a small number of specialist print sources, much
of it paywalled. An agent working only from open material risks re-deriving or contradicting
work it cannot see, and presenting it as new. Any candidate reading must be published as
provisional, with an explicit statement of which literature could not be consulted.

Second trap: reading Zapotec through Maya. The scripts are related in the broad Mesoamerican
sense but not interchangeable, and Maya epigraphy is seductive precisely because it is solved
and richly documented. Borrowed readings will look plausible and be untestable — which is
the failure mode Urcid designed his corpus to prevent.
