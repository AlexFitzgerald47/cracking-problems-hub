# The Byblos Syllabary (Pseudo-Hieroglyphic Script)

## Statement
Establish what can be legitimately concluded about the Byblos syllabary — a small
corpus of Egyptian-influenced inscriptions from Byblos, usually dated to the early
second millennium BC — given that the corpus is far too small for statistical
decipherment and that every proposed decipherment to date has failed to convince.
The realistic target is not translation but a rigorous audit: which structural claims
survive scrutiny, and which proposed decipherments can be positively excluded.

## Why it belongs on the board
Byblos is the sharpest available test of an uncomfortable methodological question the
Hub will face repeatedly: **what is the honest epistemic ceiling on a corpus this
small?** With roughly fourteen to sixteen inscriptions and nearly a hundred distinct
signs, the sign-to-token ratio is catastrophic. Multiple full decipherments have
nonetheless been published, mutually incompatible, each internally satisfying to its
author.

That makes this valuable in a way translation would not be. A defensible demonstration
of what *cannot* be concluded — and a method for exposing why over-fitted decipherments
of tiny corpora feel convincing — is directly transferable to Phaistos, to the Dorabella
cipher, and to half the board. The script's position matters too: it sits between
Egyptian hieroglyphs and the early alphabet, so its dating and affinities bear on the
origin of alphabetic writing.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- Corpus: about fourteen to sixteen inscriptions, mostly on bronze spatulae and stone,
  first found by Maurice Dunand from 1929. Nearly one hundred distinct signs attested.
- Archaeological contexts are unclear, and the dating is genuinely elusive. Benjamin
  Sass (2019) has argued for affinities with early Phoenician inscriptions, which would
  imply a substantially later date than the conventional early-second-millennium
  placement. This dating question is arguably more tractable than the decipherment and
  is where an agent should start.
- Major decipherment attempts: Dhorme; George Mendenhall (1985), reading an archaic
  North-West Semitic language via hieroglyphic and early alphabetic connections;
  Jan Best, via Linear A and peripheral Akkadian; Giovanni Garbini, connecting the
  spatulae to later Phoenician spatulae. None commands assent.
- Recent computational work exists on word-boundary detection in the corpus — a
  properly scoped, modest question of the kind this corpus can actually support.

## Success criteria
1. A reproducible structural audit: sign inventory with documented variant-merging
   decisions, positional statistics, and an explicit power analysis stating what a
   corpus of this size can and cannot support.
2. Positive exclusion of one or more published decipherments, by demonstrating that
   their sign-value assignments are internally inconsistent or fit no better than
   matched random assignments.
3. Progress on the dating question, by systematically comparing sign forms against
   dated Egyptian and early alphabetic comparanda.
4. A stated, defensible ceiling: what would have to be found for decipherment to become
   possible.

## Notes
Corpus size: ~14–16 inscriptions, ~100 distinct signs. **No open machine-readable corpus
is known to exist**; Dunand's editions and subsequent publications are the base, and
the sign inventory itself is contested.

Difficulty: decipherment, effectively impossible on present evidence. Audit and dating,
moderate. Tractability with text/compute alone: fair — the corpus is tiny enough to
transcribe fully, which is both the opportunity and the whole problem.

Time-waster warning: do not attempt a fresh decipherment. With ~100 signs over ~16
inscriptions you can fit almost any language to this corpus, which is precisely why so
many people have. Any agent that produces a new reading here has almost certainly
fooled itself, and should treat that outcome as evidence of method failure.
