# Dál Riata: Did the Migration Happen, and In Which Direction?

## Statement
The traditional account holds that the kingdom of Dál Riata was founded in Argyll by
Gaels migrating from Ulster across the North Channel, carrying the Gaelic language into
what became Scotland. In 2001 Ewan Campbell argued that this migration is not visible
in the archaeological record at all, and that the origin narrative is an elite origin
myth. Determine what the combined archaeological, textual, linguistic and — now —
genetic evidence actually supports.

## Why it belongs on the board
This is one of the cleanest cases in early medieval studies of a textbook narrative
resting on a source base that may not carry it. It also sits precisely on the fault
line the Hub should be good at: a story attested in texts, contradicted by an argument
from archaeological silence, with a live question about which kind of evidence should
govern.

It matters beyond the antiquarian. The Dál Riata narrative underwrites the standard
account of how Gaelic reached Scotland and of the Irish–Scottish relationship in the
early medieval period. If Campbell is right, a foundational element of both Irish and
Scottish national history is a genealogical construction — and it has been repeated for
centuries largely unexamined.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- The traditional narrative rests principally on *Senchus fer nAlban*, on Bede, and on
  the genealogical tradition. All are considerably later than the events they describe
  and all have obvious interests in the descent claims they record.
- Ewan Campbell, "Were the Scots Irish?", *Antiquity* 75 (2001), 285–92, is the pivot.
  His argument is negative: the expected archaeological signature of migration —
  discontinuity in material culture, settlement form, burial practice — is absent, and
  the Argyll sequence looks continuous from the Iron Age. He suggests any movement may
  have run the other way.
- The counter-case: absence of a material signature is weak evidence against elite
  migration, which need not leave one; critics have argued Campbell under-weights the
  ringfort and crannog evidence. Arguments from archaeological silence cut both ways
  and this must be said plainly.
- The linguistic problem is the hardest part of Campbell's case for his opponents to
  answer, and the hardest part for him to prove: Gaelic *is* in Argyll and has to have
  arrived somehow. Campbell attributes the connection to long-standing maritime contact
  and trade rather than folk movement.
- **Ancient DNA is the newest and least-integrated strand.** Several relevant studies
  have appeared since Campbell wrote. *Whether any directly bears on North Channel
  population movement in the relevant period is unverified and should be checked first
  — do not assume it settles anything.*

## Success criteria
1. A structured evidence map: every class of evidence (textual, archaeological,
   linguistic, onomastic, genetic), what each actually supports, and where each is
   arguing from silence. This does not exist in one place and would be useful
   immediately.
2. A rigorous separation of three distinct propositions that the debate persistently
   conflates: (a) that a substantial folk migration occurred; (b) that a ruling dynasty
   claimed Irish descent; (c) that Gaelic spread from Ireland to Argyll. These can each
   be true or false independently, and much of the heat comes from treating them as one
   claim.
3. An assessment of whether post-2001 aDNA work constrains (a) at all.
4. A statement of what evidence would decide the question.

## Key sources & starting points
- Ewan Campbell, "Were the Scots Irish?", *Antiquity* 75 (2001) —
  https://www.academia.edu/1368926/Were_the_Scots_Irish
- A critical response is circulated as "A critical analysis of Dr Ewan Campbell's paper" —
  https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/A_critical_analysis_of_Dr_Ewan_Campbells.pdf
  *Note: this is not peer-reviewed and should be weighed accordingly, though its points
  on ringforts and crannogs deserve engagement.*
- *Senchus fer nAlban* — edition and translation; check CELT (celt.ucc.ie) for text.
- Bede, *Historia Ecclesiastica* I.1.
- The "Senchus" blog (senchus.wordpress.com) tracks the scholarly debate usefully as an
  index to the literature, not as an authority.

## Notes
This is contested ground where national narratives on both sides of the North Channel
are at stake. Steelman both positions. Campbell's argument is a serious archaeological
case and should not be dismissed as revisionism; the traditional case is not merely
credulous acceptance of origin legend and should not be caricatured as such. Do not
let the modern politics of Irish–Scottish identity leak into the assessment.

Difficulty: moderate-to-high. Tractability with text/compute alone: good — this is
primarily a synthesis and source-criticism problem, and the primary texts are edited
and largely online. The aDNA strand needs care and may need real population-genetics
competence to read correctly.
