# Moynagh Lough I-MEA-003 — cracking results, session 1

## Bottom line

No complete plaintext is established. The session nevertheless changes the live explanation set in two important ways.

1. **`PIBAN` should no longer be treated primarily as the common noun “small tube”.** A seventh-century Irish personal name **Pípán / Pipan** is independently attested: Fáilbe, eighth abbot of Iona (d. 679), was *mac Pípáin*, “son of Pípán”. That date lies directly inside the antler tine’s archaeological horizon (roughly AD 625–810). The spelling history of the pipe-word itself also shows intervocalic voicing (*pípa* > forms with `b`), so the B in `PIBAN` is not an obvious obstacle to the name comparison. On a portable personal craft object, an owner/maker/name interpretation therefore has a materially stronger prior than the published discussion allowed.

2. **Side 1 has a physically motivated segmentation that happens to yield `COLOR`.** The published reading is `COLORRS`, but Stifter independently reports that the final two letters look cut with a finer blade, perhaps the same tool used for side 2. If that observation reflects a genuine second carving episode, the first episode ends after five signs: `COLOR`. That is exactly Latin *color* “colour / appearance”. Because the putative boundary is selected by reported tool morphology rather than by dictionary search, this is more interesting than an arbitrary five-letter substring. It remains only a hypothesis: no independent evidence yet explains why a leather-working antler tool would carry Latin *color*, and multilingual short-string coincidence is dangerous.

The current preferred working model is therefore **two potentially independent inscriptions or carving episodes, with at least one personal-name hypothesis live**, rather than a single confidently translated object label.

## Evidence fixed before interpretation

### Object and context

- Worked red-deer antler tine, about 63.5 × 19 mm; NMI RIA 1889:13 / I-MEA-003.
- Moynagh Lough crannog horizon approximately AD 625–800/810.
- Object re-identified in 2025 as a leather-working edge beveller/burnisher.
- One inscription uses a natural ridge; the other a deliberately drawn stem-line.
- Modern OG(H)AM examination/recording was undertaken in November 2023 using RTI/3D-derived documentation.
- Published observers explicitly report differences in cut quality and allow different tools, people or times.
- The order of the two sides, and whether they form a continuous text, is not established.

### Published sign families

Side 1:

- preferred direction: `COLORRS`
- reverse: `CRRODOS`
- fifth `R` poorly executed; sixth `R` may be an improvement/correction
- last two letters reportedly appear to be made with a finer blade

Side 2:

- preferred: `PIBANSNAVQE`
- final damaged sign can potentially be `I`
- initial sign can potentially be iphín `P/PH`, an X-forfid, or a non-phonetic start marker
- published reverse: `ENTAQCQAHIP`

The accompanying enumerator preserves 120 combinations of line form, correction/phase treatment, damaged-sign choice and side relationship instead of silently choosing one.

## Finding 1 — `PIBAN` has a period-correct personal-name competitor

### Independent attestation

The Dictionary of Irish Biography records Fáilbe (d. 679), eighth abbot of Iona, as **son of Pípán**. Historical lists also transmit him as *Failbe mac Pipan*. This is unusually useful evidence because:

- it is not inferred from the Moynagh inscription;
- the name is Irish;
- its date is essentially contemporary with the object;
- it occurs in the Irish Sea ecclesiastical world during the exact archaeological horizon.

A modern biographical tradition also gives the father’s name as **Píobán**, showing that the `p/b` development is not foreign to the name’s transmission.

### Why this matters

Stifter’s tentative `PIBAN SNAVQE = “a tube of bark”` begins by treating `PIBAN` as Middle Irish *pípán* / Modern Irish *píobán*, a diminutive of *pípa*. But the same phonological form is independently an early personal name. Thus the first half of the proposed phrase is **not uniquely lexical**.

The name hypothesis is also structurally plausible on a portable object. Irish portable ogham objects can carry personal/owner names: the Ballyspellan brooch bears multiple names generally interpreted as owners, and other portable ogham material includes personal-name readings.

### What it does *not* solve

`SNAVQE` remains unexplained. Several possibilities remain:

- a second name, byname or affiliation;
- a genitive or qualifying expression;
- a common noun;
- a second inscription rather than continuation of `PIBAN`;
- a misread sequence caused by one or more of the uncertain signs.

Therefore **`PIBAN = Pípán` is a strong new component reading, not a full decipherment.**

### Prediction

If the owner/name model is right, comparison with early Irish personal-name corpora should produce a historically plausible continuation or segmentation of `SNAVQE` without requiring the full stack of special spelling rules used by the bark phrase. If exhaustive onomastic search fails, the gain is limited to the identification of `PIBAN` as an available name.

## Finding 2 — physically selected `COLOR` on side 1

The lexical coincidence `COLOR` was *not* found by scanning arbitrary substrings. The published physical observation is that the final two signs of `COLORRS` look finer and may share the tool used on side 2. That observation defines a candidate phase boundary after position 5 independently of Latin.

Under the straightforward reading of the first five signs:

`C O L O R`

is exactly Latin *color*.

### Why it is worth keeping alive

- Latin literacy in Ireland is unquestionably available in the seventh/eighth-century horizon.
- Ogham and Latin literacy coexist, and later manuscript ogham is deeply entangled with Latin grammatical learning.
- The phase boundary has a physical rationale rather than a lexical one.

### Why it is *not* yet a solve

- No independent functional reason has been found for the word *color* on a leather-working antler tool.
- The earliest surviving manuscript evidence for learned/scholastic ogham is later than the beginning of the tine’s date window.
- A five-letter Latin word can occur by chance once multiple scripts, languages and segmentations are searched.
- We have not independently measured the groove profiles; we are relying on the specialist’s published observation.

### Strong falsifier

RTI/3D or microscope inspection should compare groove width, depth, V-profile and cutting direction for signs 1–5 versus signs 6–7 and side 2. If all seven side-1 signs have one continuous tool/profile and sequencing, the physically motivated `COLOR | RS` hypothesis loses most of its force. If signs 6–7 cluster mechanically with side 2 instead, it becomes substantially stronger.

## Reassessment of published “tube of bark” reading

Stifter’s proposal remains ingenious but is no longer preferred in this session.

It requires:

1. `PIBAN` = common noun *pípán* “small tube” rather than the independently attested personal name;
2. segmentation after `PIBAN`;
3. `SNAVQE` = hypothetical genitive `*snamchae` from a bark/cork word;
4. `V` to represent lenited `m` /β̃/;
5. `Q` to represent `C` after the /kʷ/ > /k/ merger;
6. unmarked lenition of `ch`;
7. a semantic explanation for why “tube of bark” appears on a **solid antler leather-working tool**.

None of these is individually impossible. Their conjunction is expensive, and the newly recovered name competitor removes the uniqueness of the first step.

## Reassessment of reverse side 1 and `RODDOS`

The public ogham readings corpus contains `DUGENNGG[I] MAQI RODDOS`; `RODDOS` is independently discussed in specialist literature as an ogham personal-name form. This gives the reverse `CRRODOS` family a real comparator.

It does **not** produce an exact match. `CRRODOS` has an initial `C`, adjacent `RR`, and only one `D` where `RODDOS` has `DD`. Stifter’s suggestion that one adjacent R may be a correction can reduce one discrepancy, but not all of them. Keep this as a lower-ranked image-audit lead rather than a decipherment.

## Null / overfitting discipline

This inscription is short enough that lexical resemblance is cheap. The enumerator makes 120 explicit structural hypotheses once five side-1 forms, eight side-2 forms and three side-relationship models are admitted.

Therefore the two interesting findings above are ranked by **external constraint**, not by word-likeness:

- `PIBAN ~ Pípán` gains weight from a dated independent historical person/name and portable-object naming practice.
- `COLOR` gains weight only insofar as the independently reported tool change truly selects the first five signs as a carving phase.

Any future dictionary/name search must use the same branch/search budget for its null controls.

**Open — advanced, not stale.**
