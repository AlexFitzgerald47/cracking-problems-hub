# Authorship of "The Night Before Larry Was Stretched"

## Statement
**The named unknown:** who wrote "The Night Before Larry Was Stretched," the Dublin
Newgate-cant execution ballad first attested in print by 1789 (*The Festival of Anacreon*,
7th edition, Part 2, p. 177; later undated editions 1790–91)? No candidate has ever been
established, and the leading modern reference on canting-song literature says so in as many
words: John S. Farmer, editing the poem in *Musa Pedestris: Three Centuries of Canting Songs
and Slang Rhymes, 1536–1896* (London, 1896), states that "neither the song's date nor its
author were definitely known."

A genuine crack here is a defensible attribution — or a defensible, evidenced elimination of
one or more of the standing candidates — reached by comparing the ballad's language, cant
vocabulary, meter and rhyme practice against attested writing by each candidate, rather than
by repeating whichever name the last retelling happened to prefer.

## Why it belongs on the board
This is Irish, it is genuinely obscure (a well-read Hiberno-English or broadside-ballad
specialist would know it; a general audience would not, despite the song's continuing folk-
and session-music popularity), and it sits in the early-modern/modern Ireland lane the board
has been trying to fill. It is not a demographic-estimation project — the brief's explicit
exclusion for that lane — it is a clean authorship dispute with named competing candidates,
which is exactly the crack-fit shape the discovery brief asks for.

It is also a useful test case for the Hub's own recent methodological lesson. The Shakespeare
and Junius folders have just demonstrated that period and register confounds can swamp an
authorship signal if a stylometric comparison is not matched for genre; this ballad's
candidates span wildly different registers (a possible shoemaker-poet, a cathedral dean's
formal writing, a barrister's political oratory), so the project is a live rehearsal of that
exact discipline on a small, low-stakes corpus before it is applied anywhere higher-stakes.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- **Farmer (1896)** surveyed the attribution evidence available to him and reported: "According
  to the best authorities, Will Maher, a shoemaker of Waterford, wrote the song. Dr. Robert
  Burrowes, Dean of St Finbarre's [sic], Cork, to whom it has been so often attributed,
  certainly did not." This is the earliest scholarly-style adjudication found, and it already
  rejects the most commonly repeated traditional attribution (Burrowes). *Verified* — read
  directly at `https://words.fromoldbooks.org/Farmer-MusaPedestris/the-night-before-larry-was-stretched.html`
  and cross-confirmed via the Wikipedia article's citation of the same passage.
- **The Universal Songster** (1828 edition) attributes the song to a "'Curren,'" possibly the
  barrister-politician John Philpot Curran, or a different, unidentified "J. W. Curren."
  *Reported by Wikipedia's article on the ballad; the primary 1828 volume itself has not yet
  been checked by hand — mark unverified at the primary-source level.*
- **James N. Healy, *Ballads from the Pubs of Ireland*** reportedly names "William Maher
  (Hurlfoot Bill)" as a candidate. *Unverified — full citation (publisher, year, page) not yet
  located; do not treat this as confirmed until the actual volume is found and checked.*
- **Hector McDonnell, *The Night Before Larry Was Stretched* (Belfast: Blackstaff Press, 1984,
  ISBN 0856403202)** is a real, illustrated modern edition of the poem — *confirmed to exist*
  via bookseller records (AbeBooks, Amazon). Whether it argues for a specific author, or is
  purely an illustrated reprint, is **unverified**; this book has not been read and no claim
  about its contents should be assumed.
- No stylometric or systematic textual-comparison study of this attribution question was found
  in any search covering 2000–2026. The dispute appears to sit exactly where Farmer left it in
  1896, unrevisited by modern methods.
- One of the ballad's companion "Newgate cant" execution songs from the same milieu (*The
  Kilmainham Minuet*, *Luke Caffrey's Ghost*, *Larry's Ghost*) may supply comparanda in the
  same genre and register — useful because the Hub's own confound work says genre-matched
  controls, not just any writing by a candidate, are what make a comparison meaningful.

## Success criteria
1. **A genre- and period-matched comparative analysis** (stylistic, lexical/cant-vocabulary,
   and metrical) testing the ballad against attested writing by each live candidate — Curran's
   speeches and verse (extensively published and digitized), and Burrowes's sermons or other
   writing if locatable (to test whether Farmer's rejection is confirmable on textual grounds,
   not just asserted) — with an explicit null/control built from other canting songs of the
   period so a false positive from genre alone cannot masquerade as an attribution.
2. **A resolved sourcing question for Will Maher**: does any attested writing by a "Will Maher,
   shoemaker of Waterford" survive at all? If none can be located, the correct "crack" is not
   a forced attribution but an evidenced statement that Maher is structurally untestable by
   authorship methods and the case for him rests on oral tradition alone — a legitimate
   negative result under this Hub's rules.
3. **Located and read the actual 1828 *Universal Songster* passage and the McDonnell 1984
   edition**, so the "Curren" attribution and any argument McDonnell makes are confirmed or
   corrected at the primary-source level rather than carried forward as Wikipedia paraphrase.

## Notes
Difficulty: moderate. Tractability with text/compute alone: **good** — no archive travel is
needed. The period printings and comparanda for Curran are widely digitized; the open work is
locating and transcribing the specific 1789/1828 printings (not modern retranscriptions) and
determining whether any exemplar of Maher's or Burrowes's writing actually exists to compare
against.

**Time-waster warning.** The most likely way to burn a session on this is to treat "tradition"
or the internet's default answer (usually Curran or Burrowes) as already-adjudicated: Farmer
explicitly rejected Burrowes in 1896, so any analysis that does not engage that rejection is
redoing work already discredited over a century ago. The second trap is a false positive from
register mismatch — Curran's surviving corpus is large, politically formal, and stylistically
flexible; matching it loosely against a Newgate-cant ballad without genre-matched controls
(other canting songs, not just any Curran prose) will manufacture confident-looking nonsense,
exactly the failure mode this Hub's Shakespeare and Junius sessions have already measured.
Third: if no writing sample for Maher exists, do not manufacture a comparison text or force an
attribution — say plainly that he is untestable and report that as the finding.
