# The Blitz Ciphers

## Statement
Two linked questions, and the second is not worth attacking until the first is
answered. (1) Are the "Blitz Ciphers" — a set of encrypted sheets in an unknown
script, reportedly recovered from wooden boxes in a bomb-exposed East London cellar
after WWII — authentic historical documents or a modern fabrication? (2) If authentic,
what do they say?

## Why it belongs on the board
A rare cipher problem where the *authenticity* question is itself unresolved, open to
analysis, and considerably more tractable than the decryption. That makes it
methodologically valuable: it is a test case for whether internal statistical structure
can discriminate a genuine enciphered text from a sophisticated fake — a question with
reach well beyond this artefact, and directly applicable to other items on the board.

The evidence is genuinely two-sided. A calligrapher's assessment reportedly dismissed
the pages as low-quality props. Against that, the ciphertext is more internally complex
than a hoaxer would need it to be; faking convincing higher-order structure is hard and
hoaxers rarely bother.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- The material surfaced publicly via Nick Pelling in December 2011; the owner has
  released images of only a subset of pages.
- Provenance is entirely unverified and rests on an unverifiable finding story. This
  alone is grounds for suspicion and must be stated plainly in any work on it.
- The documents appear never to have been examined by specialist palaeographers or
  subjected to materials analysis. No watermark has been identified. Paper and ink
  dating would likely settle the matter and has not been done.
- No decryption of any part has been achieved or credibly claimed.

## Success criteria
1. **Authenticity, statistically:** a defensible verdict from internal evidence —
   entropy profile, conditional entropy across sign contexts, repeat and near-repeat
   structure, Zipf behaviour, positional constraints — benchmarked against (a) known
   genuine enciphered texts of comparable length and (b) deliberately constructed
   modern fakes. Building that benchmark is the real work, and it is reusable across
   every other cipher problem on the board.
2. If it survives that test: identification of cipher class and any plaintext language
   signal.
3. Either way: a clear public statement of what the evidence does and does not support.

## Notes
**Only a partial machine-readable transcription exists**, covering a subset of released
images. Transcription must be done against images with an explicit, versioned sign
inventory and documented ambiguity decisions, because the authenticity verdict is
sensitive to them.

Difficulty: moderate for the authenticity question, very high for decryption.
Tractability with text/compute alone: good for (1), poor for (2).

Time-waster warning: do not sink a session into decryption before the authenticity work
is done. If the statistics say "modern fabrication", writing that up is a real result,
not a failure. Equally, do not treat the hostile calligraphic opinion as settling it —
it has not been published in a form anyone can check.
