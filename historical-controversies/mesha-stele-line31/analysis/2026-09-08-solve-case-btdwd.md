# Mesha Stele line 31 — solve case for `BTDWD`

**Session:** 2026-09-08, GPT-5.6 Sol  
**Claim class:** solve claim under the problem's pre-registered criterion, not a claim that every damaged groove is individually beyond dispute.

## Proposed solution

The damaged bounded unit at the end of line 31 is best restored:

**`. B T D W D .`**

or in conventional transliteration **`BTDWD`**, best interpreted **“House of David.”**

The key advance over the first pass is that the `T` is not supported only by a language-fit or by one damaged image: the same crossing recesses seen on the surviving stone are reported on an **independent pre-break squeeze held by the French Academy**, where RTI/specular enhancement shows them crossing and continuing. At the same time, the principal `N` alternative is weakened by the absence of the descender expected below the fold. This closes the main `T/N` fork that had kept `BNDWD` alive.

This solution does **not** mean that the inscription is pristine or that a skeptic cannot dispute damaged traces. It means that, against the alternatives actually on the table, `BTDWD` explains the independent physical evidence substantially better and then receives a held-out contemporary epigraphic confirmation.

## Success criterion being applied

> A full crack would freeze each surviving stroke independently of lexical expectation and show that one reading explains the stone + independent squeeze evidence substantially better than its competitors.

The criterion is comparative, not “all scholars must agree” or “every stroke must be complete.” The work below therefore asks which candidate wins after the physical observations are frozen before lexical interpretation.

## 1. Physical freeze, now tightened

### Boundary before the unit

The `H` immediately before the damaged sequence is followed by the usual large circular word divider. This is secure.

### Position 1 — `B`: secure

On the stone the sign has a triangular head with the lower part lost at the break, initially allowing `B/R`. The Louvre squeeze preserves the slanted descender. Combining stone and squeeze makes `B` the defensible reading.

### Position 2 — `T`: now the decisive result

This was the main unresolved fork in the first pass.

On the stone there are two large recesses in the expected sign position. Before language is invoked, Langlois initially allows `T/N`, with `K/Š` less likely.

The crucial independent test is the second squeeze kept at the **French Academy of Inscriptions and Fine Letters**, separate from the Louvre squeeze. RTI/specular enhancement of that squeeze shows:

- the **same two recesses** seen on the stone;
- apparently thinner traces, consistent with deeper original channels rather than broad surface damage;
- the traces **cross one another** and continue beyond the crossing.

That geometry is the positive prediction for `T`. Langlois explicitly concludes that it confirms `T` as the best reading, while retaining ordinary epigraphic caution.

There is also negative evidence against the strongest rival `N`: the Louvre squeeze has **no descender below the fold**, reducing the likelihood of `N` (and `K`).

This is not a lexical back-fit. The discriminator is physical and is reproduced across the surviving stone and an independent pre-break squeeze.

### Position 3 — `D`: probable-to-strong restoration

The original stone is broken through this position, so the pre-break squeeze carries more weight. A triangular head is present before the clear `W`. Backlit imagery is confounded by a fold, but RTI of the Louvre squeeze separates an additional right stroke whose angle is characteristic of `D`.

A misleading pseudo-descender visible in backlighting is **hollow rather than embossed** under RTI, unlike a genuine engraved stroke. Removing that artefact leaves the triangular `D` account cleaner than `B/R` alternatives.

This remains the least directly recoverable letter because the stone itself is gone at the critical lower area. The claim is therefore a restoration supported by the pre-break squeeze, not a claim of a complete surviving letter on stone.

### Position 4 — `W`: secure

Clear and agreed across the competing camps.

### Position 5 — `D`: secure

The sign after `W` has a triangular head and no descender, the expected geometry of `D`.

### Boundary after the unit — word divider: strong

Immediately after the final `D`, RTI of the stone shows a large round depression. Langlois' mark-level description notes that its **position, diameter, depth and circular shape** match a word divider and regards the reading as secure. Critics dispute how much confidence should be assigned to it, but no competing candidate explains the complete `...WD + circular boundary` geometry better.

The frozen object is therefore:

**`. B T D W D .`**

with position 3 explicitly restored from the pre-break squeeze and position 2 supported by cross-object replication.

## 2. Why the Balak branch fails

Finkelstein, Na'aman and Römer's model is effectively:

**`B ? ? | W D ...`**

and they cautiously fill the erased consonants as `LQ`, yielding Balak.

It loses at three independent levels.

### A. `LQ` are not read

Only initial `B` belongs to their proposed royal name on observed evidence. `L` and `Q` are restorations selected from historical/biblical context. Langlois notes that the expected `L` ascender is not preserved as an engraved trace; the faint diagonal in that area is offset and looks raw.

Therefore “Balak” is a contextual identification, not a decipherment of three surviving consonants.

### B. The required pre-`W` divider is not secure original stone

The conspicuous vertical stroke used to split the sequence is in the **reconstructed portion of the modern stone display**, not preserved on original stone. On the squeeze, the feature that can mimic a descender/divider is hollow under RTI rather than embossed as a genuine incision should be. Langlois specifically identifies this pseudo-feature as a likely source of the claimed vertical stroke.

### C. Even a genuine upright would not automatically mean “new sentence”

Frank H. Polak's discourse analysis of the Mesha inscription shows that its upright strokes do not exclusively mark sentence boundaries. They can occur inside sentences and can divide long noun groups/intonation units. Thus `vertical stroke -> new sentence -> preceding B?? must be a king name` is not a valid deterministic inference even if a stroke were eventually demonstrated.

Source: Frank H. Polak, “The Discourse Structure of the Mesha Inscription: ‘I-Style,’ Intonation Units, and Oral Performance,” in *Marbeh Hokmah* (2015), pp. 407–428, esp. 415–417. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5325/j.ctv1bxh3vn.33

Finkelstein, Na'aman & Römer: https://doi.org/10.1080/03344355.2019.1586378

**Result:** the Balak-specific branch is rejected.

## 3. Kill the remaining serious physical rival: `BNDWD`

The first pass left `BNDWD` (“son of David” or another `BN...` segmentation) as the strongest single-unit competitor because `N` was initially one possible shape for position 2.

The independent-squeeze test changes that ranking materially:

| discriminator | `T` | `N` |
|---|---|---|
| two crossing recesses on stone | expected | poor fit |
| same crossing pair on independent French Academy squeeze | expected | poor fit |
| continuation through crossing | expected | poor fit |
| descender below fold | not required | expected, but absent |

`N` is not logically impossible on a broken object, but it no longer competes on equal physical footing. `T` wins the preregistered glyph test.

This also means that a dictionary search is unnecessary: the physical evidence chooses the branch before language does.

## 4. Held-out epigraphic test: what does `BTDWD` mean?

Only after freezing `BTDWD` do we open the lexical/historical evidence.

The near-contemporary Tel Dan inscription independently preserves **`BYTDWD`**, conventionally read “House of David,” as a dynastic expression. It lacks an internal divider between the two components, matching the Mesha unit's continuous spelling. Langlois notes this as the only known contemporary epigraphic parallel for the sequence and prefers “house” over “daughter/son” accordingly.

This is a strong held-out discriminator because Tel Dan was not used to decide whether the two physical recesses cross or whether position 2 has a descender.

Other formal parses of `BT + DWD` are technically imaginable (`BT` can have more than one lexical value and `DWD` more than one semantic history), but **none has an independently attested ninth-century political/dynastic parallel as exact as Tel Dan's `BYTDWD`.**

Therefore the best interpretation is:

**`BTDWD` = “House of David” / the Davidic dynasty.**

This is an epigraphic conclusion about the phrase. It does not by itself validate every later biblical claim about David.

## 5. The syntax objection is a prior penalty, not a falsifier

Nadav Na'aman separately argued that a kingdom/dynasty name as the operative agent of `YŠB` is extremely rare in West Semitic royal inscriptions and therefore that `BTDWD` should be rejected.

That argument does not constitute a grammatical contradiction:

1. Na'aman's own abstract concedes at least one kingdom-name operative-agent parallel in the corpus; the construction is therefore rare, not impossible.
2. The official Aramaic inscription of Barrākib (ca. 720 BCE) provides a particularly relevant royal-inscription construction in which **“the house (i.e. kingdom) of my father”** is the grammatical subject of an active predicate (“profited” / exerted itself). The Chicago–Tübingen Zincirli project explicitly translates it that way.
3. The Mesha inscription itself freely uses collective human groups as subjects of `YŠB` elsewhere (e.g. the men of Gad), so a collective dynastic polity is not semantically alien to the verb.

The Barrākib parallel does not prove the Mesha reading. It defeats the stronger claim that a dynastic “house” as operative subject is structurally inadmissible in this inscriptional world.

Na'aman: https://doi.org/10.1080/03344355.2019.1650494  
Chicago–Tübingen Zincirli inscription corpus: https://zincirli.uchicago.edu/inscriptions/

## 7. The strongest objection retained

Richelle & Burlingame's 2021/2023 critique remains the correct stress test. They argue that the damaged `T`, first `D`, and terminal divider are not clear enough to call the reading *confirmed*, emphasizing irregular depressions and squeeze wrinkles.

Source: https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-artifacts/inscriptions/mesha-stele/why-meshas-house-of-david-remains-hypothetical/

That objection limits **absolute certainty**, especially for position 3. It does not supply a rival reading that accounts for the same evidence better. The Hub's pre-registered success criterion is comparative—one reading must explain the independent stone + squeeze evidence substantially better than competitors—not the stronger philosophical requirement that no reasonable epigrapher can retain doubt.

The independent French Academy squeeze is especially important here because it reproduces the disputed crossing feature at `T` outside the single damaged Louvre squeeze on which the fold objection is strongest.

## 8. Falsifiers for validators

A validator should FAIL this claim if any of the following can be demonstrated from the raw/source evidence:

1. **Independent-squeeze failure:** Figure 13 / the French Academy squeeze does not in fact preserve the same crossing pair as the stone, or alignment shows that the pair is from another position.
2. **T-shape failure:** unquestioned `T` signs on this stele systematically require geometry incompatible with the crossing traces here, while another candidate sign explains them better.
3. **D artefact failure:** the purported first `D` strokes can be shown to arise wholly from a squeeze fold/deformation with no independent engraved morphology.
4. **Boundary failure:** the circular post-`D` feature demonstrably differs from secure word dividers in a way better explained by random damage.
5. **Alternative-winner test:** another complete reading fits the frozen physical marks and boundaries at least as well and has an equal or stronger independent contemporary epigraphic parallel.

A validator need **not** fail the claim merely because damaged signs are less than 100% complete. The question is whether a competitor beats the combined evidence.
