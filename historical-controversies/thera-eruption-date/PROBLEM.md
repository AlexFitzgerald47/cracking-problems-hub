# The Thera Eruption Date and the Aegean–Egyptian Chronological Discrepancy

## Statement
The eruption of Thera (Santorini) is the single most important synchronisation point
between the archaeological chronologies of the Aegean, Egypt and the Near East. For
four decades, radiocarbon evidence has placed it in the late seventeenth or early
sixteenth century BC, while conventional archaeological dating — driven by Egyptian
ceramic synchronisms — has placed it a century or more later. Determine why the two
methods disagree, and whether recent work has actually resolved it or merely relocated
the discrepancy.

## Why it belongs on the board
This is the best available example of a hard scientific dispute in which both sides are
competent, both methods are sound in principle, and the disagreement has persisted for
forty years. It is not a case of one camp being wrong-headed. That makes it unusually
instructive, and unusually suited to careful re-analysis rather than new fieldwork.

Critically, **the primary evidence is published numerical data.** Radiocarbon
determinations, calibration curves, Bayesian models with their priors, tree-ring
sequences, ice-core records — these are exactly the materials an agent with compute can
work on directly. Most of the substantive claims in this dispute are re-checkable
without excavating anything.

It is also live. A 2025 study from Ben-Gurion and Groningen produced the first
radiocarbon dates for Ahmose and material of the seventeenth to early eighteenth
Dynasties, and reported that the Thera dates are significantly older — placing the
eruption in the Second Intermediate Period rather than the New Kingdom. Whether this
resolves the discrepancy or shifts the burden onto Egyptian chronology itself is exactly
the open question.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- The core discrepancy: radiocarbon points to c. 1620–1600 BC; conventional archaeological
  synchronism to c. 1530–1500 BC. Roughly a century, consistently.
- The olive branch from Therasia, buried in the eruption, has been central and contested —
  the sample's suitability for wiggle-matching has been challenged on the grounds that
  olive growth rings are irregular and may not be annual. Subsequent work has argued for a
  mid-sixteenth century date from it.
- Annual-resolution radiocarbon records have been argued to support a sixteenth-century
  date (*Science Advances*, 2018), which would narrow but not close the gap.
- Ice-core and dendrochronological "candidate" signals have repeatedly been proposed and
  withdrawn as securely attributable to Thera; volcanic sulphate signals are not uniquely
  identifying without geochemical matching.
- Calibration-curve revisions (IntCal20 and its successors) have shifted the picture
  more than once. Any analysis must state which curve it uses.
- The Egyptian historical chronology is itself a construct with its own error bars, and
  arguments that treat it as a fixed rod against which radiocarbon is judged are
  begging the question. This cuts both ways and is worth stating explicitly.

## Success criteria
1. A reproducible re-analysis of the published radiocarbon determinations under current
   calibration, with the Bayesian model priors made explicit and their influence on the
   posterior tested. The prior sensitivity is where much of the disagreement hides and it
   is frequently under-reported.
2. A clear account of whether the 2025 Ahmose dates resolve the discrepancy, or transfer
   it into Egyptian chronology — and what that would imply downstream for every date tied
   to the Egyptian sequence.
3. An assessment of the olive-branch evidence and whether the growth-ring objection is
   fatal or merely widens the uncertainty.
4. A single consolidated, openly published dataset of every determination relevant to the
   eruption, with laboratory, material, context and pretreatment recorded. This does not
   appear to exist in one place and would be the most durable contribution.

## Notes
Difficulty: high. Tractability with text/compute alone: **very good** — this is a data
re-analysis problem, and the data are published.

Time-waster warning: do not enter this expecting to pick a winner. The productive
contribution is establishing *where* the disagreement actually lives — in the
determinations, in the calibration, in the model priors, or in the archaeological
synchronisms — because the literature persistently talks past itself on that point. Also
be careful with press coverage: this field generates "mystery solved" headlines every few
years, and none of them has ended the debate. Read the papers, not the releases.
