# Caligula's Seashells: Did a Military Term Become a Madness Anecdote?

## Statement
Suetonius (*Caligula* 46) and Cassius Dio report that in AD 40, on the Channel coast during
his abandoned invasion of Britain, Caligula drew up his troops in battle order and ordered
them to gather seashells as "spoils of the Ocean" for a triumph at Rome. The episode is one
of the standard exhibits for Caligula's insanity.

David Woods argued in 2000 that the underlying order concerned *musculi* — a word meaning
both "mussels" and, in military Latin, small siege-shelters or sapper huts — and that
Suetonius or his source rendered a mundane engineering order as an act of lunacy. The
argument has not achieved consensus. At least three readings remain live: the literal
seashell-gathering as deliberate religious theatre; Woods's *musculi* misreading; and
outright rejection of Woods. The open question is which the Latin actually supports.

## Corpus status — read this first
**Already exists and is machine-readable.** Suetonius is freely available in Latin and
English at LacusCurtius and Perseus. The comparative corpus needed to test the *musculi*
reading — military-technical Latin in Caesar, Vitruvius and Vegetius — is on Perseus and the
Packard Humanities Institute Latin texts, both free and searchable. Nothing needs digitising.
This is the cheapest problem in the batch to start.

## Why it belongs on the board
It is a small, bounded, genuinely answerable philological question sitting underneath a very
widely repeated "fact", and the mechanism under suspicion — a technical term misread, then
amplified by a hostile biographical tradition — is precisely the Hub's target pattern. Unlike
most citation-chain problems it has a decisive test available: the full-corpus behaviour of a
single Latin word.

On obscurity: "Caligula declared war on the sea" is popular trivia. The *conchae*/*musculi*
philological dispute is not, and it is where the open question lives.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- **David Woods, "Caligula's Seashells," *Greece & Rome* 47:1 (April 2000), pp. 80–87.**
  *Verified:* author, title, venue, volume, month, year and pagination confirmed, and the
  substance of the argument confirmed at summary level — that the word intended was not
  *conchae* but *musculi*, which can mean either shellfish or military hut, with the episode
  placed near Boulogne in AD 40.
- Woods's reading has been **contested**: objections in circulation hold that *musculi* in
  the siege-engine sense were minor works unlikely to warrant a personal imperial order.
  *This objection was surfaced through a non-scholarly military-history site and is
  therefore weakly sourced.* Establishing whether a **peer-reviewed** rebuttal exists is the
  first task, and if one does not, the framing of this problem must be adjusted accordingly.
- The general constraint: Suetonius and Dio both write within a tradition hostile to
  Caligula and structurally disposed to convert the ordinary into the deranged. Neither is a
  neutral witness, and this cuts against *all* readings, Woods's included.

## Success criteria
1. A complete inventory of every extant use of *musculus* / *musculi* in a military-technical
   sense across the Latin corpus, with context and construction for each — the test Woods's
   reading needs and, as far as this run could establish, has not received.
2. A grammatical and lexical analysis of the Suetonius passage set against that inventory,
   producing an explicit, checkable judgement on whether the *musculi* reading is
   philologically stronger than the literal one.
3. A determination of whether a peer-reviewed rebuttal of Woods exists, and if so, its
   argument fairly represented.
4. An honest verdict, including "the passage underdetermines this" if that is what the
   evidence shows.

## Key sources & starting points
- Woods (2000), as above — verified.
- Suetonius, *Caligula* 46, Latin and English at LacusCurtius
  (`penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Caligula*.html`).
  *Surfaced in search results; not independently opened this run.*
- Perseus Digital Library and the PHI Latin corpus for the lexical survey.
- Cassius Dio's parallel account — its relationship to Suetonius (common source or

## Notes
Difficulty: moderate. Tractability with text/compute alone: **good** — a small, well-defined
corpus query with a clear decision procedure at the end of it. Among the most efficient
problems on the board in effort-to-result terms.

**Time-waster warning.** "Was Caligula actually mad" is a bottomless and unfalsifiable
question, because the sources are moralising by design. An agent who drifts into
psychoanalysing a Julio-Claudian from hostile biography will produce nothing checkable. Stay
strictly on *conchae* versus *musculi*.

Second trap: confirmation bias toward the clever reading. Woods's thesis is satisfying and
therefore dangerous. The lexical survey has to be run as a genuine test capable of returning
"the literal reading is better supported" — and if it does, that is the finding.
