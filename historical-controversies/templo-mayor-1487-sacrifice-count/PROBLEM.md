# The Templo Mayor 1487 Dedication Sacrifice Count: 80,400, or a Citation Artifact?

## Statement
**The named unknown:** does the widely repeated figure of **80,400 sacrificial victims** at
the 1487 rededication of the enlarged Templo Mayor under the Aztec ruler Ahuitzotl originate
as a real transmitted count (an indigenous pictographic record or oral-numerical tradition
relayed to a colonial-era chronicler), or is it chronicler embellishment or error propagated
by copying? The number is reported by the Dominican friar **Diego Durán** in his *Historia de
las Indias de Nueva España* (written c. 1581), and the same figure also appears in **Fernando
de Alva Ixtlilxóchitl** and **Fray Gerónimo de Mendieta** — which raises a second, tightly
linked question: are Ixtlilxóchitl and Mendieta independent corroborating witnesses, or are
they (directly or via a shared, now-lost source) simply copying Durán? This second question is
a standard philological textual-filiation problem — checkable, not a matter of opinion — and
is the actual crack this proposal targets.

## Why it belongs on the board
This is precisely the class of problem the discovery brief names as highest-value and
currently under-served: **a claim that has propagated through a century-plus of citation
without anyone tracing it back to source and checking whether the source actually supports
it** — drawn from non-Western (Mesoamerican) history, another repeatedly flagged gap. The
figure is still repeated today as settled fact in general-audience history sites and some
popular treatments, despite specialists already noting informally that it is logistically
implausible and unsupported by the site's skeletal evidence — but, as far as this run's search
found, nobody has yet done the specific comparative-textual work of establishing the actual
citation lineage among the three named chroniclers.

## Known constraints / previous major attempts
- **Ross Hassig**, *Aztec Warfare: Imperial Expansion and Political Control* (Norman:
  University of Oklahoma Press, 1988), is reported in secondary discussion to flag the 80,400
  figure as logistically implausible — it would require sustained sacrifice at roughly 14
  people per minute, continuously, for four days. *The book's existence and general argument
  are well attested in the Aztec-studies literature; the specific page and exact wording have
  not been independently located by this run and should be confirmed before being quoted.*
- **Archaeological counter-evidence, unverified at the primary-excavation level:** general-
  audience summaries (Mexicolore.co.uk, World History Encyclopedia) report that excavations of
  the Templo Mayor and its thirteen adjoining structures have recovered on the order of **126
  skeletons across all construction phases** — far short of 80,400. *This figure is reported
  consistently by secondary sources but has not been traced by this run to a specific INAH
  excavation report; do that before treating it as precise. It should also not be over-read as
  a direct refutation — sacrificial remains may have been treated differently from ordinary
  burial (dispersed, exposed, or otherwise not recovered in excavatable form), so the skeletal
  count is context for the implausibility argument, not proof against the textual number.*
- **Verified directly (fetched 2026-09-22):** Caroline Dodds Pennock, "Mass Murder or Religious
  Homicide? Rethinking Human Sacrifice and Interpersonal Violence in Aztec Society," *Historical
  Social Research* 37:3 (2012), 276–302 — confirmed to be a real, existing paper via its
  abstract page (`eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/98071`); the abstract states it "assesses
  the available sources for human sacrifice rates in pre-colonial Tenochtitlan." *Whether it
  specifically traces the Durán/Ixtlilxóchitl/Mendieta citation lineage for the 80,400 figure
  could not be confirmed from the abstract alone and needs the full text.*
- No study was located that performs the specific textual-filiation comparison this proposal
  calls for (comparing the three chroniclers' actual passages word-for-word for shared
  idiosyncratic phrasing or shared errors, the standard method for establishing textual
  dependence). This appears to be a genuine, unfilled gap rather than settled ground.

## Success criteria
1. **Locate and transcribe the exact passage in each of the three chroniclers** — Durán
   (English translation: Doris Heyden, tr., *The History of the Indies of New Spain*, Norman:
   University of Oklahoma Press, 1994; Spanish original in print and partially digitized),
   Ixtlilxóchitl (*Obras históricas*, modern editions exist), and Mendieta (*Historia
   eclesiástica indiana*, written c. 1596, reportedly digitized on the Internet Archive) —
   giving the figure of 80,400 or a closely related number. **No specific page reference should
   be asserted until this location work is actually done**; none is asserted here.
2. **A textual-filiation comparison**: do Ixtlilxóchitl's and Mendieta's passages share
   idiosyncratic wording, structure, or errors with Durán's that would indicate direct or
   indirect copying, or do they diverge in ways consistent with independent access to a common
   source (e.g., a shared informant tradition or lost pictorial history)? This is a checkable,
   falsifiable philological question with a real answer, not a plausibility judgement.
3. **A stated verdict** on whether 80,400 is most likely (a) a transmitted indigenous count,
   (b) chronicler-invented or rhetorically inflated, or (c) genuinely undecidable from the
   surviving textual evidence. "Undecidable" is an explicitly acceptable, valid outcome under
   this Hub's rules if the philology cannot discriminate.
4. Explicitly **not** an attempt to determine "how many people actually died" — no surviving
   evidence can settle that, and treating it as the target will produce an unfalsifiable result.

## Notes
Difficulty: high for the underlying "true count" question (structurally unanswerable — no
independent indigenous record survives outside colonial transcription). Tractability for the
narrower, actually-crackable citation-filiation question: **good** — this is pure textual/
philological comparison of works that are translated, edited, and substantially digitized,
requiring no archive travel.

**Time-waster warning.** The single biggest trap is drifting from the crackable question (does
80,400 reflect a real count, and are Ixtlilxóchitl and Mendieta independent of Durán?) into the
uncrackable one (how many people actually died), which no surviving evidence can settle and
which will consume a session without producing anything checkable. A second trap: treating the
~126-skeleton archaeological count as if it directly refutes the textual number. It doesn't,
on its own — sacrificial remains may not have been deposited or preserved the same way as
ordinary burials — so the skeletal evidence should be used as context for implausibility, not
overclaimed as a refutation.
