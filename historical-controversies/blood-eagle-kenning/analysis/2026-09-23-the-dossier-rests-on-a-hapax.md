## 1. What changed after the measurements

The base-rate file was written against Frank's argument **as characterised in `PROBLEM.md`**,
and flagged that as its main debt. That debt is now cleared. This session obtained and read
Frank's own words in two places, and Bjarni Einarsson's reply, and the reading changes the
write-up in two ways — one that strengthens the result and one that takes credit away from it.

Both were fetched and read by me directly, not via a researcher:
`https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Saga-Book-XXII.pdf` and
`…/Saga-Book-XXIII.pdf` (Viking Society for Northern Research; free, open, and it was a
delegated researcher's report that these volumes did *not* contain the exchange — they do).

### 1a. Frank's construal is confirmed, and it is the one with base rate zero

> "My article affirmed that, although Ella's back might just possibly have been incised with
> the picture of an eagle ('And Ívarr, who dwelt at York, had Ella's back cut **with** an
> eagle'), it was far more likely to have been lacerated by a real one ('Ívarr had Ella's back
> cut **by** an eagle')."
>
> — Roberta Frank, "The Blood-Eagle Again," *Saga-Book* XXII:5 (1988), p. 287.

She restates it in 1990:

> "I have argued (English historical review 99, 1984, 332-43) that a half-stanza by Sighvatr
> Þórðarson may allude to the eagle as carrion beast, the bird with red claws perched on and
> lacerating his victim's back: 'Ivarr had Ella's back lacerated (or torn, cut, carved) **by an
> eagle**.'"
>
> — Frank, "Ornithology and the Interpretation of Skaldic Verse," *Saga-Book* XXIII (1990–93),
> pp. 81–82.

So `ara` is a **prepositionless dative of agent**: the eagle does the cutting. That is
construal C2 in the base-rate file. Its measured rate in the skaldic corpus is **0 of 772
beast-of-battle occurrences**, in both OCR scans, with all 35 candidate co-occurrences
adjudicated row by row. The conventional alternative — the beast as recipient or patient of a
feeding or colouring verb — runs at 14.6–19.5 %.

And she does call it conventional, in the sentence Einarsson quotes back at her:

> "an experienced reader of skaldic poetry, looking at Sighvatr's stanza in isolation from its
> saga context, would have trouble seeing it as anything but **a conventional utterance**, an
> allusion to the eagle as carrion beast, the pale bird with red claws perched on and slashing
> the backs of the slain." — Frank 1988, p. 287, quoting her own 1984 article.

That is the premise the measurements test, and it fails.

### 1b. Bjarni Einarsson got here first, in 1986, and must have the priority

This is the part that takes credit away, and it belongs at the top rather than in a footnote.

> "The main reason is that use of the verb *skera* is inconceivable in any context where
> carrion beasts rip into dead bodies by claw, tooth and neb. **In the old poetic language
> *skera* invariably refers to cutting with a knife or sword or, in general, with weapons or
> some sharp tool.** By transfer it is also often used of ships cutting through waves…
> You would have to say that the bird *sleit* or *át*, 'tore' or 'ate', the dead flesh. These
> are the verbs used in skaldic poetry for describing what eagles and ravens, and wolves too,
> do to corpses: *átu ernir af jǫfurs dolgum; brátt gat hrafn at slíta hold; sleit ǫrn gera
> beitu; brátt brátt hafði at slíta hrafn tafn* (Skj. B I 496, 257, 452, 491). …
> It follows that the expressions *skera* and *bak* in Sighvatr's lines are **both unsuitable**
> in a description of the natural behaviour of carrion birds."
>
> — Bjarni Einarsson, "De Normannorum atrocitate, or on the execution of royalty by the
> aquiline method," *Saga-Book* XXII:1 (1986), pp. 79–82, at 80.

Einarsson asserted, with four citations and a native speaker's judgement, exactly what this
session measured: that `skera` is not a carrion-beast verb, that `bak` is the wrong body part,
and that the conventional verbs are `slíta`/`eta`. He even cites the treading formula — Snorri,
*Háttatal* 51, `ilspornat getr ǫrn aldrlausastan haus` (Skj. B II 75) — which is the §4 formula
of the companion file.

**The idea is his. What this session adds is the number**, and in a dispute that was settled by
counter-example that is not a small addition: Frank answered his four examples with three of
her own (below), and four-against-three is not an argument anyone can win. 0 out of 772, with
an OCR replicate, an adjudication table and a power statement, is a different kind of object.
Add to that two things Einarsson did not have: the complete `bak` enumeration (21 occurrences,
76 % idiomatic, one co-occurrence with a beast and one with a blade verb, both the disputed
passage) and the Torf-Einarr control.

### 1c. Frank's three counter-examples do not touch the skaldic claim

Einarsson's claim was about **"the old poetic language."** Frank's rebuttal:

> "Bjarni will have to have a word with the author of *Stjórn* (ca 1310), who seems unaware of
> this prohibition. Indeed, this anonymous writer uses *skera* three times to describe the
> cutting action of claws. Panthers, he explains, give birth only once, because the cubs' claws
> tear their mother's womb…: [they] '*skera svá ok slíta allan hennar kvið meðr sínum klóm ok
> allan getnaðarliminn*' ('thus they cut and slash all her womb and genitalia with their
> claws'; *Stjórn*, ed. C. R. Unger, 1862, 80/11 … **the words in italics translate Isidore's
> *unguibus lacerant*, Etym. XII, i, ii**)." — Frank 1988, p. 288.

All three instances are from one passage of one text. That text is:

- **prose**, not skaldic verse — so it does not meet Einarsson's claim, which was about the
  poetic language, and which the companion file measures in the poetic language;
- **translated** prose: Frank herself says the words render Isidore's Latin `unguibus lacerant`.
  A calque is the weakest available evidence for native idiom;
- **c. 1310** — roughly 270 years after Sigvatr, and later than every prose blood-eagle account
  except the latest;
- about **panther cubs in the womb**, not carrion birds on a battlefield;
- and two of the three are doublets (`skera ok slíta`, `skerast ok skemmast`) — which is
  Einarsson's stated objection, and Frank's answer to it ("not persuasive") is an assertion.

Her fourth argument is dialectal — "Present-day Icelanders may not be able to say *hræfugl skar
ná*, but their brothers in Norway still can and do." Modern Norwegian is not evidence about
eleventh-century skaldic diction either.

**So Frank's defence of the `skera` reading contains no skaldic evidence at all.** The number
that would settle it did not exist. It does now: zero.

## 2. The metrical defence, and a test of it

Frank anticipated the objection that the stanza never mentions claws, and answered it:

> "Viking-age skalds were not reluctant to see men falling under the eagle's talons; **the
> ellipsis in Sighvatr's verse — the skald's omission of claws — reflected, I argued, the
> demands of his terse metre (called tøglag)**, a speciality of Cnut's Anglo-Scandinavian
> court." — Frank 1988, p. 287.

This is an auxiliary hypothesis, and it is testable. Note first that Frank's own sentence
identifies the conventional formula as *"men falling under the eagle's talons"* — which is
precisely the formula the companion file finds, ≥5 times across ≥4 poets, and which Sigvatr
does not use.

**The test.** Tøglag lines carry four metrical positions. If the metre forced the claws out,
then a poet in a metre at least as tight should also have to drop them. *Ynglingatal* is in
kviðuháttr — 3 positions in odd lines, 4 in even — which is tighter, and it is the oldest
skaldic poem we have. Þjóðólfr ór Hvini, st. 19:

| | line | positions |
|---|---|---|
| Ynglingatal 19 | `Fell Óttarr` | fell / Ótt / arr = 3 ✓ |
| Ynglingatal 19 | `und ara greipar` | und / ar(a) / greip / ar = 4 ✓ (with resolution of the short `ara`) |
| Knútsdrápa 1 | `Ok Ellu bak` | ok / Ell / u / bak = 4 |
| Knútsdrápa 1 | `Ívarr, ara` | Í / varr / ar / a = 4 |

Þjóðólfr fits **eagle + talons** into four positions, in a tighter metre, a century and a half
earlier. Sigvatr spends four positions on a name and a bare `ara`. The metre does not force the
omission: a poet under greater metrical pressure managed to include exactly what Frank says
Sigvatr had to leave out.

*Stated at the confidence it deserves:* this rests on the standard treatment of resolution for
a short stressed syllable, and on the standard metrical descriptions of tøglag and kviðuháttr.
I am not a trained metrist and this is the single claim in these two files most likely to be
wrong on a technicality. It should be the second thing a validator attacks, after the
adjudication table. It is also not load-bearing — the base rates stand without it.

- *Orkneyinga*: `jall let rista blodǫrnn a baki honum med suerdi ok skera fra rifin hrygginum ok

- *Heimskringla* 31: `hann reist ǫrn á baki honum með þeima hætti, at hann lagði sverði á hol

- *Ragnarssona þáttr*: `letv þeir nv rista orn a baki Ellv ok skera siþan rifin oll fra
  ryggino m með sverði sva at þar voru lvngvn vt dregin. Sva segir Sigvatr skalld i Knvtz drapv`
- *Norna-Gests þáttr*: `rísta skyldi blóðörn á baki honum … svá at hann skar rifin frá hryggnum
  ok dró þar út lungun`
- *Orms þáttr*: `reist blóðörn á baki honum ok skar öll rifin frá hryggnum ok dró þar út lungun`
- *Ragnars saga* 17: `sá maðr, er oddhagastr er, marka ǫrn á baki honum sem inniligast, ok þann
  ǫrn skal rjóða með blóði hans`

**Three things fall out, and none of them is in the literature in this form.**

**(i) The escalation is not chronological; it is branched.** Frank's stemma says the rite
"becomes more lurid, pagan, and time-consuming with each passing century." But *Orkneyinga
saga* (c. 1200–30) is contemporary with Saxo (c. 1200) and already has the complete rite —
compound, ribs, lungs and Óðinn — while Saxo has only a wound shaped like an eagle, and
*Ragnars saga* only a marked-and-reddened outline. The full rite does not develop over time out
of the sketch. **It is present from the earliest date in the Hálfdan branch and absent from the
earliest date in the Ælla branch.** Two traditions with different content, not one tradition
maturing.

**(ii) The branches merge at *Ragnarssona þáttr*, c. 1300 — and that is the only text that
quotes Sigvatr.** It gives Ælla the Hálfdan-branch rite (ribs from the spine, lungs out) and
appends the stanza as proof. The skaldic citation enters the dossier *at the end of the chain*,
as corroboration of an already-composite story, not at its head as its source. Any argument
that treats Knútsdrápa as the root of the prose tradition has to account for the fact that no
text before c. 1300 cites it.

**(iii) The prose verb is not Sigvatr's verb.** Every prose account uses `rísta` (or `marka`,
or a Latin periphrasis) for the eagle, and reserves `skera` for a different action — `skera
rifin frá hryggnum`, cutting the ribs from the spine. Sigvatr uses `skorit` of the back itself.
If the prose descended straightforwardly from the stanza's wording, `skera` is the word it
should have inherited for the carving, and it did not.

What every account *does* share, across both branches, across Latin and Norse, across historical
and legendary victims, is the bare pair **an eagle and a back**.

## 4. The convergence, and the verdict

The prose tradition's invariant core is `ǫrn` + `bak`.

That pair occurs **once** in the entire skaldic corpus — 21 occurrences of `bak`, one of them
with a beast of battle; 772 beast occurrences, one of them with a `bak`. Knútsdrápa 1. The
invariant of the prose dossier is the hapax of the verse corpus.

This lets both sides of the eighty-year dispute be settled at once, in opposite directions.

**Frank's conclusion is right, and the corpus now quantifies it.** She wrote: *"Deprived of its
skaldic stanza, the rite of the blood-eagle has no viking-age support"* (1988, p. 289). That is
correct, and "no support" can now be given a value. Outside this one half-stanza there is not a
single viking-age poetic attestation of an eagle and a back — not one, in a corpus of 772 beast
occurrences and four volumes of verse, read twice through independent scans. Every prose witness
postdates the events by three centuries or more, and the earliest of them are already split into
two content-branches.

**Frank's argument for it is wrong, and the corpus shows why.** The stanza is not "a conventional
utterance." It is a hapax on the verb (0/772 for a beast as agent of a blade verb), a hapax on
the body part (1/21 `bak`), and it declines the conventional carrion formula that Frank herself
names in the same breath — `falla und ara greipar`, used by at least four poets including
Þjóðólfr in a tighter metre. Einarsson saw this in 1986 and was answered with a fourteenth-century
prose translation of Isidore on panthers.

**And that is why the question cannot be settled.** A hapax cannot adjudicate itself. The
comparative evidence that would tell us what `skorit … ara` means consists of exactly one
instance, which is the thing in dispute. This is not a counsel of despair and it is not the
literature's usual shrug — it is a measured statement with a denominator. The blood eagle is
undecidable from the skaldic evidence, and the reason is a number: **n = 1.**

The one substantive thing the corpus *does* decide is the relative plausibility of the two
construals, and it decides against the received sceptical reading. Frank needs the eagle to be
the agent of a blade verb, which the corpus never permits elsewhere (95 % upper bound 0.39 %).
Einarsson needs `skera` to carve a figure on a surface, which the corpus does permit — `rikula
ristin rit`, `baugvang skorinn`, `fagrt of skornir drekar`. That is not proof that a rite
happened. It is a statement that **of the two readings of the stanza, the one modern scholarship
has largely adopted is the one with no corpus support**, and that the sceptical case has been
resting for forty years on a claim of conventionality that nobody had checked.

## 5. What the Hálfdan branch independently shows — and it favours Frank

Section 5 of the companion file stands and points the other way, and it must not be quietly
dropped because the rest of this file cuts against Frank.

Torf-Einarr's own four lausavísur on the killing of Hálfdan háleggr survive, in two redactions
(*Orkneyinga saga* and *Heimskringla*, both verified this session). In them he says his enemies
will `hverr ilþorna arnar undir hlýtr at standa` — "lie under the eagle's talons" — the
conventional carrion formula, verbatim. There is no rite in the killer's own verse. *Orkneyinga
saga* had that verse in front of it (it quotes it) and produced `rista blóðǫrn á baki honum …
draga þar út lungun … gaf hann Óðni til sigurs sér`.

**In the Hálfdan branch, Frank's mechanism is not a hypothesis. It is documented input and
documented output.** And the Hálfdan branch is the one that supplied the full rite — the ribs,
the lungs, the Óðinn — to every later text, including the version of Ælla's death that
*Ragnarssona þáttr* assembled c. 1300 and then proved with Sigvatr's stanza.

So the dossier's most lurid content descends from a demonstrable literalisation, and its
supposed viking-age authority descends from a hapax. Those are two different failures, in two
different branches, and treating the dossier as one body of evidence has concealed both.

## 6. Standing debts

- **Frank 1984 (EHR) itself is still unread.** Both the 1988 and 1990 notes are Frank restating
  and defending the 1984 argument, and they are unambiguous about the construal and about
  "conventional," so the risk that the 1984 text says something materially different is low —
  but it is not zero, and pp. 335–341 (where she presumably lays out parallels) remain
  unverified. Secondary report, not independently confirmed by me, says her 1984 syntactic
  support is three prepositionless instrumental datives with past participles elsewhere in
  Knútsdrápa (Murphy et al., *Speculum* 97:1 (2022), citing EHR p. 339). I located one such
  construction myself in the poem — st. 5, `Lét lǫnd lokit … marbeðjum með mǫrg nefbjǫrgum` —
  and its datives are **instruments** (ships, helmets), not agents, which if anything supports
  Einarsson's construal. **Verifying the other two is a cheap, high-value next step.**
- **Murphy et al. (2022) has not been read by me**, only reported. Its anatomical argument is
  orthogonal to everything here; its philological summary is not, and should be checked.
- **Saxo's Latin** is from Holder's 1886 edition via archive.org OCR as relayed by a researcher;
  I have not re-read the page image. The coding of Saxo in the matrix should be treated as
  one notch weaker than the Norse rows.
- **Dates** in the matrix are conventional scholarly datings taken at face value. They are load-
  bearing for finding (i) — specifically, that *Orkneyinga saga* is not materially later than
  Saxo. If *Orkneyinga saga*'s blood-eagle passage is a late interpolation into the Flateyjarbók
  redaction (which is a fourteenth-century manuscript), finding (i) weakens badly. **This is the
  single most dangerous assumption in this file and the first thing to check.**
