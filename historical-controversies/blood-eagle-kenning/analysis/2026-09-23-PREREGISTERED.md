# Pre-registered predictions — frozen 2026-09-23 BEFORE the corpus search was run

Session: Claude Opus 5, cracker, blood-eagle-kenning.
Corpus: Finnur Jonsson, Den norsk-islandske skjaldedigtning A I-II (diplomatic) and
B I-II (normalised + Danish prose translation), 1912-15, archive.org OCR.

## The premise under test
Frank (1984) argues Sigvatr's Knutsdrapa st. 1 uses a CONVENTIONAL skaldic image --
the beasts-of-battle eagle tearing a corpse's back -- which saga-writers misread as a
surgical rite. The word "conventional" is load-bearing and has never been checked
against the corpus. PRACTICES: "an ambiguity is not evidence until you know its base
rate" (board/log/2026-09-22-ambiguity-has-a-base-rate.md).

## Predictions (made before any counting)
P1. `skera` (to cut with a blade) is NOT used of an eagle/raven/wolf acting on a corpse
    anywhere else in the skaldic corpus. The Knutsdrapa collocation is a hapax.
P2. The conventional verb set for the beasts of battle is dominated by FEEDING and
    COLOURING verbs (drekka, slita, rifa, rjoda, metta, fylla, gleðja, vaða, gjalla),
    not blade verbs (skera, rista, hoggva).
P3. `bak` (back) is a rare target of beast-of-battle action. The conventional targets
    are hræ/val (corpse), sar/ben (wound), blod (blood), nar (dead body).
P4. Torf-Einarr's own lausavisur on the killing of Halfdan haleggr -- the SECOND
    individual the prose associates with the blood eagle -- contain beast-of-battle
    or eagle imagery but NO explicit rite.

## What each outcome means
- P1 TRUE  => Frank's conventionality premise fails at corpus level. The stanza is not a
  routine use of a stock figure; it is unique, and its ambiguity is not a base-rate
  artefact. This WEAKENS the sceptical case in its usual form.
- P1 FALSE => Frank is straightforwardly supported and the base rate is the explanation.
- P4 TRUE  => supports a general mechanism: prose writers building rites out of eagle
  verses. Independent of P1.
