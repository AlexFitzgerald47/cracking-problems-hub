## Intelligence-cipher seam promoted

A fresh public-source pass found four useful leads that fit the Hub's cracking mission better than generic historical-data work:

1. **Michael Collins Papers — orphan Crown Forces/RIC ciphertext discovery.** The digitised Collins corpus includes codebreaking/intelligence material. Treat this as a finder-style mining task first: identify surviving ciphertexts, then classify each as solved, key-known, historically solved, or apparently orphaned. Only the final class becomes a standalone cracking target.
2. **IRA `VORFYDCGT`, 25 October 1923.** A nine-letter coded token appears in an IRA Director of Intelligence memo in the NLI Ernie O'Malley material: “Can any of 100's methods be used now that no VORFYDCGT?”. No published decipherment was located in the current pass. Because nine letters are statistically weak, contextual reconstruction of “100”, the memo chain, vocabulary and any internal code system is the real attack surface.
3. **NLI MS 50,046/1–143, IRA Army Council ↔ Clan-na-Gael, 1929–31.** Catalogue metadata says many messages contain code/cipher and at least one June 1931 dispatch has two coded sections fully decoded on an accompanying slip. This could provide crib/key material for other messages. Primary-image access is the current blocker; also audit Mahon/Gillogly coverage before claiming anything as unsolved.
4. **WWII British carrier-pigeon cipher.** GCHQ still treats the 27-group message beginning `AOAKN HVPKD FNFJW YIDDC` as unresolved. Do not prioritize ciphertext-only brute force. The highest-value path is historical identification of sender “W Stot”, destination/unit `X02`, system/codebook and parallel traffic.

## General lesson

For intelligence ciphers, the most important first artefact is a **solution-status ledger**. Archives often preserve ciphertext, key sheets, plaintext summaries and later decodes in different files. A message is not a Hub cracking target until a source audit shows the solution is genuinely absent or disputed.
