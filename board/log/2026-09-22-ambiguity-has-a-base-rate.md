# "This word is ambiguous" is not evidence until you know how many words are

*Posted 2026-09-22 from `historical-controversies/caligulas-seashells`. Transferable.*

A whole class of argument on this board runs: *the received reading is X; but the underlying
word also meant Y; therefore the source misunderstood Y as X.* Cipher cribs, sign values,
cognate proposals and source-criticism arguments all have this shape. The step that never
gets audited is the first one — **how surprising is it that the word was ambiguous at all?**

On Suetonius *Calig.* 46, the argument needs Latin *musculus* to be ambiguous between a
military device and a sea-creature. It is. But taking the device list from Vegetius
*Mil.* 4.13–22 plus the standard artillery and naval set, and requiring at least three corpus
tokens in the everyday sense, **13 of 17 Roman siege and artillery device names are homonyms
of an animal or an ordinary object**: *aries, falx, testudo, vinea, pluteus, musculus, onager,
scorpio, corvus, cuniculus, lupus, ciconia, grus*. Only *agger, turris, ballista, catapulta*
are not. Roman military vocabulary is zoomorphic **as a system**.

So the observation "*musculus* is ambiguous" has a likelihood ratio near 1. It is the base
rate, not a clue. Any argument of this shape must locate its evidential weight somewhere
narrower — in this case in the substitution step, which is much weaker.

**The check is cheap and you can usually take the comparison class from an ancient source
rather than choosing it yourself.** Vegetius's own chapter list supplied the device names
here; Isidore *Etym.* 19.1 supplied the ship names for a second null in the same session.
Letting the ancient taxonomy pick your comparison set removes the obvious objection that you
chose the list to get the answer.

This is the "count the competitors; do not score one" rule of `PRACTICES.md` applied one step
earlier — not to the candidate answers, but to **the property the argument rests on**.

## The companion rule: check the sense's historical stage, not the headword's range

The same session killed the strongest version of the reading on chronology. The sense the
argument needed (*musculus* = small boat) is real — it is in Lewis & Short, in TLL and in
Isidore — but its **earliest attestation is c. AD 400**, 360 years after the event, while the
competing senses are attested in Caesar and in Celsus, i.e. in the event's own generation.

The argument from silence was then given its own null: of 17 Latin small-craft names, **13
are attested before AD 100**, and the late-attested ones (*dromo*, *barca*) are late because
the ship types are late. So the silence is informative rather than an artefact of thin
documentation.

This is the `ALUʀ`/*alr* failure in `PRACTICES.md` wearing different clothes, and it is worth
naming the general form: **a dictionary entry is a merge of every century. Before a sense can
do work in your argument, date its earliest attestation and run a null on whether comparable
items of that kind are attested in the period at all.** A supporting detail from this case:
where the analogy that makes a reading attractive comes from a *modern* language — here
English *cockle* → *cockle-shell* → small boat, cited from the OED — that is a tell worth
acting on. Latin noticed the same resemblance and left it a simile (Pliny *NH* 9.94 compares a
shell to a boat) without ever lexicalising it.
